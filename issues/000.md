On October 6 OpenAI [posted](https://openai.com/index/sharing-ai-progress-in-mathematics/) about 720 manuscripts, grouped into 372 result families, written by an internal model it has not released. The company says it gave the model roughly 4,000 open problems from the literature and published what came back. About 42 percent of the top-line results arrived with proofs checked in Lean, the formal language in which a proof either compiles or it doesn’t. The rest is ordinary mathematical prose, and none of it has been peer reviewed.

The reactions split along a familiar line. “If a human did this, it would be an instant Fields Medal, no questions asked,” [wrote](https://officechai.com/ai/mathematicians-react-with-shock-and-wonder-after-openai-releases-over-300-math-proofs-at-once/) Alex Kontorovich of Rutgers. Constantin Kogler of the IAS [called it](https://officechai.com/ai/mathematicians-react-with-shock-and-wonder-after-openai-releases-over-300-math-proofs-at-once/) “completely superhuman mathematics.” Mark Braverman of Princeton [told Quanta](https://quantamagazine.org/as-ai-closed-in-on-unique-games-proof-researchers-raced-to-beat-the-machines-20261007) that “math by press release is not that healthy for math,” and the Association for Human Mathematics urged colleagues to stop working with OpenAI. Terence Tao [described the release](https://fortune.com/2026/10/07/openai-math-controversy-solutions-370-outstanding-challenges-published-criticisms-celebration/) as the end of “Math 1.0”, the era in which mathematics is organised around solving problems.

Within days at least three results [had been withdrawn](https://fortune.com/2026/10/07/openai-math-controversy-solutions-370-outstanding-challenges-published-criticisms-celebration/). That is the honest state of things: an avalanche of claims, a fraction of them formally checked, and a field deciding in public what counts as a proof when no person wrote it. We will return to it when the reviews arrive.
